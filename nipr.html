Case study · Enterprise product

Turning a fragmented
licensing process into
one unified experience.

NIPR plays a critical role in the U.S. insurance industry, helping professionals and organizations navigate producer licensing and registration across the country. The platform exists to make a complicated process easier, but over time the digital experience itself had become part of the complexity. I worked with NIPR’s internal designers and developers to rethink it from the ground up.

Client
NIPR
Year
2024
Scope
UX Strategy · IA · Design System
Channels
Website · Licensing application
The system contained the information. The user had to assemble the experience.

The challenge

A platform designed around the system instead of the user.

NIPR handles inherently complicated workflows. Insurance licensing involves different requirements, jurisdictions, information, applications, statuses, and steps. The platform had grown over time to support those needs, but much of the experience had been designed and built from a technical perspective rather than around a cohesive UX strategy.

The result was a dated interface and a highly fragmented experience. In some workflows, users could end up with as many as 14 NIPR screens open at the same time just to accomplish a single task. They were responsible for understanding how those screens related to one another, remembering what they had already completed, and figuring out what needed to happen next.

Start by understanding the system

Before we could simplify it, we had to understand everything inside it.

We couldn’t simply redesign the screens. The first challenge was understanding exactly what NIPR had, so I worked with the team to inventory and categorize the existing experience.

  • Pages
  • Components
  • Interface elements
  • Content types
  • Forms
  • Actions
  • Navigation patterns
  • Statuses
  • Repeated functionality
  • Variations of similar components

We needed to understand what every element did, what information it contained, and why it existed. That gave us a map of the system we were actually redesigning. Only then could we begin asking:

  • What belongs together?
  • What’s duplicated?
  • What’s missing?
  • What does the user actually need?
  • What can disappear?

Sketchin Sessions™

Get designers, developers, and domain experts solving the problem together.

Rather than disappearing, designing a solution, and returning with an answer, I wanted the people who understood the system deeply involved in defining the new experience. We worked through sections of the platform together.

We sketched ideas, challenged assumptions, and progressively created a shared understanding of what the new experience needed to become.

  • What was this part of the experience trying to accomplish?
  • What information was missing?
  • What did users need to understand here?
  • What functionality was essential?
  • What could be simplified?
  • What should happen next?

Reorganizing around the user

The company’s structure shouldn’t become the customer’s navigation.

Over time, digital products often begin reflecting the systems, teams, databases, and processes behind them. But users don’t care how an organization stores information. They care about completing their task.

I worked to reorganize the experience around the user’s journey rather than the underlying technical architecture.

Instead of asking

Where does NIPR keep this information?

We asked

When does the user need this information?

That distinction influenced everything from navigation and content hierarchy to workflows and dashboard design.

The defining idea

Fourteen screens became one experience.

Previously, completing a licensing task could require users to navigate between numerous NIPR screens and keep multiple windows open simultaneously. We brought those fragmented interactions together into one unified experience. Instead of making the user manage the process themselves, the product could guide them through it.

  • One task.
  • One experience.
  • A clear sequence of steps.

Make the process visible

Tell people where they are and what comes next.

Simplification wasn’t only about reducing screens. It was about reducing uncertainty. Licensing can involve multiple steps, and those steps aren’t always immediately understandable to someone completing the process. So we made the sequence explicit.

  • Where you are.
  • What you’ve completed.
  • What you’re working on now.
  • What’s left.
  • What happens next.

Steps could be numbered, tracked, and surfaced directly within the experience. Instead of asking users to remember their progress, the product remembered for them.

The dashboard

Turn complexity into a clear plan of action.

Rather than simply acting as a collection of links into different parts of NIPR, the dashboard could give users a clear understanding of their licensing activity.

  • Active applications
  • Completed steps
  • Current step
  • Remaining steps
  • Application status
  • Required actions
  • Important information
  • Next steps

The goal was to make the dashboard answer a few basic questions immediately.

  • What am I working on?
  • Where am I in the process?
  • Is there anything I need to do?
  • What’s next?

Experience design

Simplify without pretending the process is simple.

Insurance licensing is complicated. Good UX doesn’t mean hiding that complexity. It means organizing it. I worked through the experience architecture, flows, wireframes, components, interactions, and interface hierarchy to make complicated workflows easier to understand.

  • Clear hierarchy
  • Progressive disclosure
  • Consistent components
  • Predictable interaction patterns
  • Visible progress
  • Strong status communication
  • Reduced navigation
  • Fewer disconnected screens
  • Clear next actions

The goal wasn’t to make licensing look easy. It was to make the process understandable and manageable.

A unified design language

One product should feel like one product.

The initial inventory exposed another challenge. Years of development had produced different components, patterns, and ways of presenting similar information. That inconsistency made an already complicated experience harder to learn.

We established reusable patterns that could work across both the public-facing site and the application experience.

  • Common actions look and behave consistently
  • Statuses communicate consistently
  • Forms follow predictable patterns
  • Navigation logic doesn’t change between workflows

Consistency became a form of simplification.

Working with development

Design the experience with the people who have to make it real.

This wasn’t a project where design happened in isolation and was thrown over the wall. I worked for months alongside NIPR’s internal designers and developers, and that collaboration was critical.

Development brought

  • The technical systems
  • Business rules and dependencies
  • The realities underneath the product

Design brought

  • The user journey
  • Hierarchy and interaction model
  • The experience perspective

We needed both. Together we could determine where the existing architecture could support the new experience, where compromises were necessary, and where larger changes were worth making.

From website to product

Create one connected NIPR experience.

NIPR had both informational website needs and application workflows. To the user, those aren’t necessarily separate products. They’re simply different moments in accomplishing the same goal.

Learn → Understand requirements → Begin → Complete steps → Track progress → Finish

That meant treating the website and application as parts of one broader customer journey.

The result

From fragmented screens to a guided licensing experience.

The transformation wasn’t simply visual. We changed the underlying experience model.

Before

Up to 14 disconnected screens, and the user had to understand how the pieces connected.

After

One unified sequence, with progress visible and next actions clear.

  1. Step 1
  2. Step 2
  3. Step 3
  4. Step 4
  5. Complete

Progress was visible. Completed work was accounted for. Next actions were clear. And the dashboard provided a central place to understand what was happening. We moved the cognitive burden away from the user and into the product.

The bigger idea

A complicated process doesn’t require a complicated experience.

Sometimes the underlying system genuinely is complicated. There are regulations, business rules, different jurisdictions, and technical dependencies. UX can’t make those realities disappear. But it can decide who carries the complexity.

Before the redesign, much of that burden sat with the user. They had to understand how the pieces connected, keep track of their own progress, and navigate the organization’s systems. Our job was to move as much of that burden as possible into the experience itself.

  1. Inventory
  2. Understand
  3. Reorganize
  4. Simplify
  5. Guide

The licensing process was still complex. Using NIPR didn’t have to be.
