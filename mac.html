Case study · Connected retail

Reimagining beauty
retail as a connected
experience.

MAC has always been a brand built around artistry, experimentation, and human connection. I worked on the experience design for MAC’s concept store in Queens, New York, helping create a connected physical-digital journey that let customers explore products, virtually try them on, save their preferences, and carry those choices with them throughout the store.

Client
MAC Cosmetics
Scope
Experience Design
Channels
In-store · AR · RFID
Location
Queens, New York

About this work: I led this in a director role at a previous agency, before founding Artifact Digital. The problem, the decisions and the outcomes are described as they happened.

The goal wasn’t simply to add technology. It was to rethink what the relationship between customer, product, artist, and store could become.

The challenge

Bring digital into the store without losing what makes MAC human.

Beauty retail has always been highly physical. Customers want to see colors, touch products, experiment, try something unexpected, and, importantly, interact with professional makeup artists who understand how those products come together.

MAC wanted to modernize that experience without replacing the human interaction that made the brand special. The opportunity was to create a retail environment where physical and digital behavior could work together.

  • A customer could explore digitally.
  • Interact physically.
  • Save what they discovered.
  • And bring that into a conversation with a MAC Artist.

A retail lab

Design something that could learn.

The Queens concept store wasn’t intended to simply be another MAC location. It was designed as a retail laboratory: an environment where new technologies and interaction models could be introduced to real customers, observed, measured, refined, and eventually considered for broader rollout.

That meant I wasn’t designing around the assumption that every idea was already correct. The store itself became part of the design process.

  1. Create
  2. Launch
  3. Observe
  4. Learn
  5. Improve

MAC Pass

Give the customer a memory.

Customers could identify themselves by scanning a personal QR code at experiences throughout the store, including the Lips, Eyes, and Face stations. Once connected, the store could begin remembering what the customer discovered.

Instead of every interaction existing independently, those moments could accumulate into a persistent customer profile. That created a very different kind of retail experience. The store could remember you.

  • Products
  • Shades
  • Favorites
  • Selfies
  • GIFs
  • Virtual looks

Designing continuity

Let choices follow the customer.

The real power of MAC Pass wasn’t simply logging someone into an experience. It created continuity. A customer might discover a lipstick at the Lips station. That choice could then become part of what they explored at Eyes. Their eye choice could contribute to a complete Face look.

Instead of interacting with disconnected screens throughout the store, customers could progressively build an understanding of what worked for them. The store began behaving more like a continuous journey than a collection of individual touchpoints.

Discover → Save → Build → Refine

Experience design

Make sophisticated technology feel like play.

A major part of my role was making sure these experiences didn’t feel technical. The customer shouldn’t need to understand any of what was running underneath.

  • RFID
  • Augmented reality
  • Customer identity
  • Cloud infrastructure
  • Data persistence
  • Connected profiles

They should simply understand three things.

  • Pick something up.
  • See what it looks like.
  • Save it if you love it.

The interface needed to give customers enough information to make decisions while keeping the overall experience playful, intuitive, and entertaining. Beauty is experimentation. The digital experience needed to feel the same way.

Virtual Try-On

Experiment without the friction.

Using augmented reality, customers could preview products directly on themselves through digital mirrors and connected devices, experimenting with lip colors, eye shades, looks, and product combinations without having to physically apply every product.

That removed friction from experimentation. Try something. Try something completely different. Take a selfie. Create a GIF. Save a favorite. Share it. Move on. The goal wasn’t to replace trying makeup physically. It was to make discovery faster, broader, and more fun.

Tap & Try

Pick it up. See it on.

A customer picked up a physical MAC product. RFID recognized which product they were holding. The connected virtual mirror immediately applied that product digitally to the customer’s face. No searching, no typing, no scrolling through a catalog.

The technology underneath it was sophisticated. The interaction wasn’t. That’s exactly why it worked.

  • Pick up a physical product
  • RFID recognizes it
  • The mirror applies it instantly
  • Save it, or put it back and try another

Pick up the product → see the product on you.

Physical → digital → physical

Blur the boundary.

Tap & Try represented a bigger idea behind the entire experience. I wasn’t designing a digital product that happened to sit inside a store. I was designing interactions between physical products and digital experiences.

  • Hold something real and see its effect digitally.
  • Decide digitally, then find or purchase the physical product.
  • Let those digital preferences help a real MAC Artist recommend better.

The boundaries between channels started disappearing. That was the real opportunity.

Don’t replace the artist

Make the expert more informed.

One of the most important design considerations was preserving the role of MAC’s professional makeup artists. Technology wasn’t the hero of MAC. Artistry was.

As customers moved through the store, MAC Pass could accumulate information about what they liked and what they had explored. That information could ultimately make the interaction with a MAC Artist better.

Instead of starting with

“What are you looking for?”

The artist could already know

Products explored, shades liked, looks created, favorites saved.

The digital experience didn’t need to replace human expertise. It could make human expertise more powerful.

Personalization

Make the experience theirs.

The connected store also created opportunities for customers to make their purchases more personal, moving away from a completely standardized retail interaction and toward an experience that increasingly felt like it belonged to the individual customer.

  • Custom product printing
  • Personalized packaging
  • Custom palettes
  • Saved favorite shades
  • Personal product combinations

The result

More than 200% higher digital engagement.

The concept store provided an immediate opportunity to see whether customers would actually engage with these new experiences. They did.

Customers responded especially well to Tap & Try because of its ease of navigation, clear shade imagery, and immediate connection to Virtual Try-On. Virtual Try-On proved engaging enough that the experience continued to expand, and custom printing became a customer favorite.

  • Ease of navigation
  • Clear shade imagery
  • Immediate connection to Virtual Try-On
  • Custom printing as a customer favorite

The bigger idea

The store doesn’t have to forget you when you walk away from the screen.

The most interesting part of the MAC project wasn’t any single interface. It was the system connecting them.

  • A physical product could trigger a digital experience.
  • A digital experiment could become a saved preference.
  • A saved preference could travel through the store.
  • Those preferences could help an artist understand the customer.
  • And the same identity could connect the store to the wider relationship with MAC.

That changes the role of digital inside retail. It isn’t another channel. It becomes connective tissue. The technology disappears into the experience, while the customer, the product, and the artist remain at the center.
