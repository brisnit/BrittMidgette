Men’s Wearhouse
Store of the Future

Men’s Wearhouse set out to rethink what shopping for menswear could look like inside its stores, giving customers better ways to discover their personal style while equipping style experts with digital tools to serve them more efficiently.

Client
Men’s Wearhouse
Year
2019
Scope
Research · Concepting · UX · Visual Design · Prototyping
Channels
In-store · Interactive retail

About this work: I led this in a director role at a previous agency, before founding Artifact Digital. The problem, the decisions and the outcomes are described as they happened.

Our challenge was bigger than designing an interface. We were exploring the future of the store itself.

The challenge

Bring digital intelligence into a deeply human shopping experience.

Shopping at Men’s Wearhouse has always relied heavily on the relationship between the customer and an experienced style expert. The opportunity wasn’t to replace that interaction with technology. It was to make it better.

Men’s Wearhouse needed to modernize its in-store experience and evolve its operating model, helping style experts understand customers faster, make more relevant recommendations, and create a more intuitive shopping experience.

We began by immersing ourselves in the business, understanding the existing customer experience, the role of the style expert, and where digital experiences could create meaningful value inside the store.

Concepting the Store of the Future

12 ideas. One question: what actually helps the customer?

Rather than jumping directly to a predetermined solution, we explored the possibilities. We developed 12 digital experience concepts designed to improve different moments throughout the customer journey.

  • Discover their personal style
  • Navigate product selection
  • Understand their options
  • Receive recommendations
  • Collaborate with style experts
  • Interact with merchandise
  • Move efficiently through the store
But these were hypotheses. The customers needed to tell us which ones were worth pursuing.

The UX think tank

Putting ideas in front of 60 real customers.

We took the concepts to a UX research think tank in Chicago and tested them with 60 Men’s Wearhouse customers. Rather than asking customers what they thought they wanted, we put concepts in front of them and observed how they responded.

Testing let us move beyond internal assumptions and narrow the original twelve down to the two strongest opportunities: the Shirt Wall and the Lay Down Table.

  • Which ideas made immediate sense?
  • Which solved an actual problem?
  • Which created confusion?
  • Which genuinely improved the shopping experience?
12 Concepts explored
60 Customers tested with
2 Validated directions

Shirt Wall

Choose your own adventure shopping.

Shirt Wall transformed what could be an overwhelming wall of merchandise into a guided, personalized discovery experience. Customers could approach it in two different ways.

Tell us your style

  • A simple five-question style quiz
  • A curated collection of shirt recommendations
  • Matched to individual taste and needs

Explore on your own

  • A unique filter wheel for customers who knew what they wanted
  • Fast navigation across ten different categories
  • Refine the selection yourself
Two paths. One simple goal: help me find shirts that feel like me.

Designing the experience

From validated concept to working product.

Once the concepts had survived customer testing, we began defining exactly how the experiences would work.

  • User flows
  • Interaction models
  • Wireframes
  • Product behavior
  • Interface design
  • Interactive prototypes

The goal was simplicity. There was significant thinking underneath the experience, but the customer shouldn’t have to see any of that complexity. The final product needed to feel obvious.

From prototype to the store

Test it where it actually matters.

A prototype working in a conference room wasn’t enough, so we took the experience back into the real world. The working prototypes were installed and tested inside the Men’s Wearhouse flagship store in San Jose, California.

Now we could observe customers interacting with the experience in the environment it was actually designed for. This was no longer an idea for the Store of the Future. It was becoming part of the store.

  • We watched
  • We learned
  • We identified friction
  • We modified the experience
  • And we tested again

The result

From 12 ideas to an experience customers could actually use.

What began as an exploration of the future Men’s Wearhouse experience became a validated digital product. It gave customers a fast, intuitive way to discover products matched to their individual style, and gave style experts another tool for creating better customer interactions. After testing and refinement in San Jose, the experience was rolled out into flagship stores across the country.

The bigger idea

Technology shouldn’t replace the expert. It should make the expert better.

The most important part of the Store of the Future wasn’t the screen. It was understanding where technology belonged in the experience.

Men’s Wearhouse already had something valuable: knowledgeable people helping customers look and feel their best. Our job wasn’t to disrupt that relationship. It was to design technology around it.

  1. Research gave us the possibilities.
  2. Customers helped us choose the direction.
  3. Design made it tangible.
  4. Real-world testing made it better.

And eventually, an idea became part of the store.
